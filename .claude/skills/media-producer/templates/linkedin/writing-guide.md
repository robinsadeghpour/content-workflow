# LinkedIn Post Writing Guide

Write posts that sound like a person talking to a friend. Not a template. Not a content generator. A real take from someone with a point of view.

The bar: if you read it out loud and it sounds like a speech, rewrite it. If it sounds like something you'd say over coffee, ship it.

---

## Voice

Declarative statements. No rhetorical questions. No dramatic fragments for effect.

Short, punchy sentences that read like conversation. If it wouldn't come up naturally talking to a friend, cut it.

**Banned phrases** — instant tells of a content generator:
- "honest answer", "compounding curiosity", "becoming invisible"
- "it's important to note", "in today's landscape", "let that sink in"
- "here's the thing", "unpopular opinion", "hot take"
- "game-changer", "unlock", "leverage", "double down"
- Any sentence that starts with "And" for dramatic effect
- Any sentence that's just one word followed by a period ("Perspective." / "Period." / "Full stop.")

If you catch yourself reaching for any of these, say the actual thought in plain language.

---

## Structure

Not every post needs all five parts, but this is the skeleton:

**1. Hook** — First 1-2 lines visible before "see more". One concrete situation, fact, or observation. Specific beats abstract. "Someone asked me today what matters most for developers in the AI era" works. "In a world where AI is transforming everything" doesn't.

**2. Take** — The core insight. Short list or direct statement. The reader clicked "see more" for this — reward them immediately.

**3. Shift** — What's changing and why it matters. Connect the personal to the structural. One paragraph, tight.

**4. Proof** — What thriving looks like, or a personal example. Show, don't explain. If the take was abstract, the proof is concrete. If the take was concrete, the proof is the pattern.

**5. Closer** — One hard-hitting consequence or conclusion. Short. Nothing after it. No CTA, no "thoughts?", no "agree?". End on the insight.

---

## Rhythm

- Vary sentence length. Long sentence sets context. Short one lands.
- Last sentence of every paragraph should be the shortest in that paragraph.
- Never explain something you already showed with an example.
- Lists work when items are parallel in length/weight. If one is longer, pull it out as its own sentence.
- Read it out loud. Where you run out of breath, break. Where it's choppy, merge.

---

## Length

Target **150-250 words**. Go to 300 only if every paragraph earns its place.

---

## Editing Checklist

Every "yes" means something needs to change:

- Is the same point made twice? Keep the sharper version.
- Does any explanation interrupt a list's rhythm? Move it after or cut it.
- Does the closing line land clean, with nothing diluting it after?
- Are there any rhetorical questions? Remove them.
- Any phrases that sound like AI output? Rewrite in plain language.
- Is the hook visible before "see more"? (First 2-3 lines only)
- Does any paragraph have more than 4 sentences? Tighten it.
- Is there a CTA at the end? Move it to comments.

## Reference Examples

### Example A — Insight-driven

> Someone asked me today what attributes will matter most for developers in the AI era.
>
> My take: the same ones that always separated great engineers from average ones. Systems thinking. Taste. Product sense. Prioritization. It's more of a founder mindset than a classic developer.
>
> The software engineer role is genuinely changing. Coding is solved. AI writes the code. What matters now is whether there's a person behind it who understands the whole picture and can direct it toward the right problems.
>
> The people who will thrive are the ones with high agency and a bias toward action. They see something new, they try it. They hear about a problem, they go figure it out. They're just constantly in it.
>
> The ones who don't develop those attributes won't just fall behind. They'll find the job itself has moved on without them.

### Example B — Fact-driven (short)

> I built a full SaaS application with a lead scraper, Supabase database, authentication, and Stripe payments. The whole thing took about 2 hours.
>
> For about 30 years, software itself was the competitive advantage. The code was the moat.
>
> That era is over.

## Common Mistakes

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| "It's important to note that coding is changing" | "Coding is solved." |
| "The ones who don't adapt will become invisible" | "They'll find the job itself has moved on without them." |
| Ending with a CTA paragraph | End on the insight |
| List item that's 2x longer than the others | Pull it out as its own sentence |
| Explaining the hook before diving in | Just dive in |
| Opening with "In today's fast-paced world..." | Open with a concrete fact or situation |
| "What do you think? Drop your thoughts below" | Nothing. The post ends on the insight. |

---

## Source Integrity

Every factual claim must trace to source material — card transcripts, NotebookLM research, or YouTube content.

**Rules:**
- Do not invent statistics, studies, percentages, or data points. If a number doesn't appear in the research, don't use it.
- Do not fabricate quotes or attribute statements to people unless the source material contains them verbatim.
- Robin's own opinions, interpretations, and takes are encouraged — just don't dress them up as external facts.
- If you want to reference a trend or shift, ground it in something specific from the research rather than making a general claim.

**Test:** For every factual statement in the draft, you should be able to point to where in the research it came from. Opinions don't need sourcing — facts do.
